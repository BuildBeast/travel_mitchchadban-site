# There & Back Again — Blog Production System

## 1. Mission

Produce a destination-cluster library that earns search traffic without turning There & Back Again into ordinary travel-blog paste.

The site is a literary travel chronicle, part guide, part memoir and part field report. Practical articles may be more decision-led than journey chronicles, but they must retain the same intelligence, restraint, specificity and atmosphere.

The governing principle is:

> Every journey is practical and mythological at the same time.

The golden rule is:

> This is travel writing that refuses to become content.

## 2. Non-negotiable provenance rules

### Personal experience

Never invent or imply that Mitch personally:

- stayed in a neighbourhood or hotel;
- ate at a restaurant;
- took a route or transport service;
- visited an attraction;
- paid a stated price;
- encountered a person or event;
- felt a particular emotion;
- travelled at a particular time or in particular weather.

First-person or traveller-narrative material is allowed only when supported by an existing article, supplied notes, receipts, photographs, tickets or an explicit statement from Mitch.

When a practical article is research-led, write as an informed editorial guide. Do not counterfeit memoir.

### Facts

Do not guess opening hours, ticket prices, journey times, timetables, closure days, reservation rules or neighbourhood boundaries. Verify unstable facts from current primary sources and record the access date in the research packet.

Where information varies, say so and link the reader to the relevant official source in the article's source notes or implementation metadata.

### Gaps

If the available material cannot support a claim, omit it or flag it for Mitch. Do not fill the silence with plausible fiction.

## 3. Voice

Primary voice: reflective literary traveller.

- Calm, observant and measured.
- Slightly old-world or mythic in cadence, never cosplay.
- Confident without breathless enthusiasm.
- Sensory when evidence supports it.
- Specific nouns over ornamental adjectives.
- Medium-to-long sentences with controlled rhythm.
- History and culture used lightly and accurately.
- The traveller records the place rather than becoming the hero of it.

Avoid:

- influencer language;
- hype and commands;
- generic superlatives;
- purple prose;
- sentimentality;
- clickbait;
- modern internet slang;
- empty scene-setting;
- listicle filler;
- repeated AI phrases such as “nestled”, “vibrant”, “tapestry”, “boasts”, “must-visit”, “hidden gem”, “bucket list”, “something for everyone” and “you won’t want to miss”.

## 4. Content architecture

Each destination should have one cornerstone itinerary and only the supporting pages justified by genuine search intent.

Typical major-hub cluster:

1. Cornerstone duration itinerary.
2. Best areas to stay.
3. One day in the destination.
4. Best things to do / top sights.
5. Best day trips.
6. Selective food, transport or specialist guides.

Do not automatically create every template for small places. A niche detour may need one excellent article, not five thin siblings.

### Cannibalisation rule

Before proposing or drafting any article:

1. Search the existing content inventory.
2. Identify the primary query and reader decision.
3. Explain why the proposed page is distinct from the cornerstone and sibling pages.
4. Define what content belongs elsewhere and must not be duplicated.

## 5. Article-type structures

### A. Cornerstone itinerary

Use for missing city-level foundations such as Seville, Granada, Marrakech and Rabat.

Required components:

- Search-facing title and literary standfirst.
- Arrival or orientation opening, based only on supported material.
- “At a glance” practical summary.
- Day-by-day route with geographically sensible sequencing.
- Food used as a cultural anchor, not bolted-on recommendations.
- Where to stay summary linking to the full accommodation guide when available.
- Getting there and around.
- Budget or cost guidance only where supportable and current.
- Tips and watch-outs.
- Further reading and internal links.

### B. Best areas to stay

This is a decision guide, not a hotel affiliate catalogue.

Required components:

- Fast recommendation table.
- Clear explanation of how the city is spatially organised.
- Five to eight areas only when the destination supports them.
- For each area: character, best for, trade-offs, walking/transit reality, evening atmosphere, relationship to major sights and who should avoid it.
- Practical booking considerations.
- Explicit distinction between neighbourhood choice and specific accommodation recommendations.
- Link to the cornerstone itinerary and relevant transport guide.

Do not claim personal stays without evidence.

### C. One day in X

This is a disciplined route, not a compressed list of every landmark.

Required components:

- Who the itinerary is for.
- Start point and finish point.
- Time-blocked route with realistic dwell and transfer time.
- Morning, lunch, afternoon and evening rhythm.
- Booking-critical items.
- Shorter, rainy-day or low-energy alternative where useful.
- Map-ready stop order.
- What was deliberately omitted and why.
- Link to the longer cornerstone itinerary.

### D. Best things to do / top sights

Use the number only when the inventory supports it. Do not force “15” if 10 or 12 produces a better page.

Required components:

- A prioritised shortlist before the full entries.
- Group attractions by geography or theme where useful.
- For each entry: why it matters, realistic time needed, booking requirements, location pairing and the kind of traveller it suits.
- Honest distinction between essential, specialist and optional sights.
- A suggested first-visit selection.
- Link to the one-day and cornerstone itineraries.

### E. Best day trips

Required components:

- Comparison table: destination, travel time, transport mode, ideal for, minimum time and principal trade-off.
- Rank by usefulness, not arbitrary fame.
- Explain DIY versus organised-tour practicality where relevant.
- Use current transport operators and realistic station-to-centre friction.
- Distinguish true day trips from places better treated as overnight stays.
- Link to existing standalone day-trip chronicles.

### F. Food guide

Required components:

- Cultural and regional food context.
- Dishes or food traditions before venue lists.
- Neighbourhood or meal-time logic.
- Venues only when adequately sourced.
- Reservation, opening-day and service-hour cautions.
- Any venue appearing in narrative prose must also appear in the practical layer.

## 6. Standard article package

Every completed article must include:

- Proposed title.
- H1.
- Slug.
- Primary search intent.
- Primary keyword and restrained secondary terms.
- Meta title.
- Meta description.
- Standfirst/deck.
- Anchor-menu labels where the site supports them.
- Article body in the repository’s actual content format.
- Internal links, with exact source and target URLs.
- Suggested external primary sources.
- Image brief and recommended source folder or existing photographs.
- Alt text grounded in the selected image.
- FAQ only when it resolves real reader questions and does not repeat the article.
- Structured-data recommendation only when supported by the site implementation.
- Factual review date.

## 7. Research workflow

For each article, create a research packet before prose.

The packet must contain:

1. Article purpose and reader decision.
2. Existing-page overlap and anti-cannibalisation boundary.
3. Personal source material found in the repository.
4. Primary-source facts with URLs, publication/update date where visible and access date.
5. Stable facts versus facts needing later refresh.
6. Geographic route logic.
7. Unsupported claims or missing information.
8. Proposed outline.
9. Internal-link map.
10. Image requirements.

Claude must stop for approval after the pilot research packet and outline. Do not produce a full pilot draft before approval.

## 8. Drafting workflow

After approval:

1. Draft in the site’s actual format.
2. Preserve existing frontmatter and component conventions.
3. Do not change shared components, styles, routing or content schemas unless the article cannot be integrated otherwise.
4. Use placeholders such as `[MITCH INPUT NEEDED: ...]` for genuinely missing personal material.
5. Run the QA checklist.
6. Report every file created or changed.
7. Do not publish, deploy or commit unless explicitly instructed.

## 9. Internal linking

Every supporting page must link:

- back to its destination cornerstone;
- to the most relevant sibling guide;
- to any existing route or day-trip chronicle it expands;
- to the appropriate country, region or archive page where such pages exist.

The cornerstone must be updated to link out to each approved supporting page.

Use descriptive anchors. Avoid repetitive exact-match anchors and “click here”.

## 10. Quality bar

Reject or revise a draft when any of the following is true:

- The prose could describe another city after swapping the name.
- The article invents personal experience.
- The practical advice is not current or sourced.
- The route is geographically foolish.
- The introduction delays the answer.
- Multiple sections repeat the same landmarks or advice.
- The article cannibalises the cornerstone.
- The tone resembles an affiliate listicle or tourism-board brochure.
- The destination does not support the proposed page depth.
- The article lacks a clear reason to exist.

## 11. Production cadence

- Pilot: one article through all stages.
- Stabilisation: one article at a time for the next two articles.
- Normal production: batches of no more than three, each with separate research packets and QA reports.
- Never mass-generate an entire destination matrix without review.
