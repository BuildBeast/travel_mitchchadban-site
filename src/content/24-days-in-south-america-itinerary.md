South America is too large to be understood from one window. It has to be crossed: from the Pacific coast into the Andes, from mountain rail to overnight road, from a city in a bowl to vineyards and waterfalls, and finally to a capital spread across neighbourhoods rather than offering one answer. Twenty-four days made the journey a line, not a loop.

The traveller began in Lima and ended in Buenos Aires, with Peru, Bolivia, Chile, Argentina, Brazil and Uruguay joined by flights, trains, buses and one day across the Río de la Plata. Each leg altered the view. A capital became a mountain town; a mountain town became an archaeological landscape; a cable car became a border road; a waterfall became two countries looking at the same river.

The crossings were part of the place: rail into the mountains, wheels through the night, cabins above La Paz and a border dividing one waterfall between two countries.

## I. The Pacific Gate

### Lima — three nights between the ocean and the old city

Lima began with layers already in collision. The historic centre gathered San Francisco, Plaza Mayor, Lima Cathedral, the Government Palace, Santo Domingo and Las Nazarenas into one civic and religious register, the grand façades and enclosed courtyards holding the city’s older authority close to the square. The next day moved from Barranco’s bridges and museums to Miraflores and the Pacific, then to Pucllana, Huaca Huallamarca and the Larco Museum, where the city’s pre-Columbian past sat inside its modern districts.

The final Lima day returned to museums and public space—MALI, the Museo de Minerales Andrés del Castillo, Parque La Muralla and Kennedy Park. The sequence kept refusing a single Lima. Church steps gave way to coastal cliffs; ancient brickwork appeared among apartment blocks; collections carried the city’s older worlds indoors. Lima was not a prologue to Machu Picchu. It set the journey in motion.

## II. Into the Mountains

### Cusco, Aguas Calientes and Machu Picchu

On 13 September, the traveller left Lima for Cusco, then continued by PeruRail towards Aguas Calientes. The mountain stage arrived in pieces: rail, steep walls, a town gathered around its tracks, and the archaeological park above it all.

Machu Picchu was the centre of the stage, but not its only subject. Two nights made room for the Aguas Calientes market, hot springs, the Butterfly Zoo and meals in town. Huayna Picchu occupied its own morning on 14 September, while the following morning returned to the National Archaeological Park before the journey turned back towards Cusco.

Below the ruins, Aguas Calientes gathered around the railway, the market and the steep walls above the river. The traveller moved from a capital of plazas and museums into a place where the train, the mountain and the ruins controlled the shape of the day.

## IV. The City in the Bowl

### La Paz — movement above the streets

La Paz changed the way the traveller moved through a city. Plaza Murillo and the Witches’ Market marked the first afternoon on 16 September. Mi Teleférico lifted the route above the streets, while Mirador Killi Killi placed the city against the folds of its valley.

The following day moved through Sucre Square, the Coca Museum, San Francisco Basilica, national museums and Mercado Rodríguez before returning to the cable-car network and Hernando Siles Stadium. Valle de la Luna came on the morning of 18 September, when the city gave way to eroded walls and paths outside the dense centre.

The city’s height stayed present in the movement between them. Walking and cable cars carried the days between markets, museums and viewpoints; the planned running gave way to walking, with easy jogging left as an option rather than a separate event.

The stadium was Hernando Siles; the day remained a city visit rather than a football-match story. Markets sat at street level, cable cars in the air, museums between them, and the valley opened beyond.

## VII. The Great Water

### Iguazú — one landscape, two countries

The Iguazú stage ran from 26 to 29 September, with Puerto Iguazú as the base. Argentina and Brazil did not offer duplicate views of one attraction. They made two different days from the same river frontier.

The Argentine side moved through Hito Tres Fronteras, Iguazú National Park, Garganta del Diablo, the Upper Circuit, the Lower Circuit and the hummingbird garden. Brazil brought the Cataratas trail, Macuco Safari and Parque das Aves; border and police stops came between them.

Argentina arranged the falls in named stages. Brazil opened the water into a wider day that continued towards birds and a butterfly. It was not a contest between better and worse sides; it was the reason the route allowed both. Iguazú became larger when the border was crossed.

## VIII. The Last Capital

### Buenos Aires and the day across the river

Buenos Aires was the final base: five nights from 29 September to 4 October. Plaza de Mayo, Casa Rosada, the Obelisk, Teatro Colón, Recoleta Cemetery, El Ateneo and Palacio Barolo formed the civic and cultural centre, a sequence of squares, institutions, theatres and monuments. San Telmo, Puerto Madero, La Boca, Palermo and the parks changed the city’s scale again.

The capital unfolded barrio by barrio. Recoleta concentrated monuments and museums; San Telmo brought markets and older streets; Puerto Madero turned towards water and newer structures; La Boca brought art, port architecture and a stadium visit; Palermo held the base and the parks. Buenos Aires did not ask to be solved by one landmark. Its meaning accumulated in the distance between districts, in the change from cemetery stone to painted walls, from port edge to park shade.

On 2 October, the traveller spent a separate day in Colonia del Sacramento before returning to Buenos Aires.

## Closing distance

By the time Buenos Aires arrived, the journey had crossed from the Pacific coast to the Andes, from a mountain railway to an overnight road, from cable cars and foothills to the two-sided water of Iguazú. Lima had set the first urban terms. Machu Picchu had turned the route vertical. In La Paz, getting around became part of the experience. Santiago and Mendoza had carried it across the continental spine.

Then the road widened again into Buenos Aires, a final capital understood district by district. Beyond that city, Colonia waited across the river as one last outward gesture before the return home.

Twenty-four days did not make South America smaller. They made its distances visible. The traveller crossed six countries and left with the road still running beyond the edge of the map.

But the road is ever onward.
