## How the route worked in practice

The route’s strength was its contrast. Lima established the urban scale. Machu Picchu shifted the journey into a rail-and-mountain stage. The overnight bus made the transition to La Paz direct and demanding in logistical terms. Santiago restored a city rhythm, Mendoza opened into parks and wine country, and Iguazú broke the route across two national sides before Buenos Aires brought it back to a large urban base.

The route also used different kinds of movement: flights, PeruRail, an overnight bus, a Santiago–Mendoza bus and a cross-border Iguazú segment. That variety is part of the journey’s identity, but it means transfer days need to be treated as real portions of the itinerary rather than invisible gaps between attractions.

What worked particularly well was the sequence from Lima into Machu Picchu, then from Santiago into Mendoza. The first moved from capital to archaeological destination; the second changed from civic city to parks and wine-country stops. The Iguazú stage also benefited from being treated as two country-side experiences rather than compressed into one generic waterfall day.
